# Agile Logbook

### Behind every screen was a journey of decisions, challenges and progress.

AquaLink was not built in a single moment.

It developed through weeks of requirements analysis, interface design, architecture planning, frontend implementation, integration work, testing and preparation for presentation.

Our Agile Logbook provides a dated record of that journey.

As Project Lead and a substantial frontend contributor, I see this document as more than an academic requirement. It represents the work, decisions and obstacles that shaped our project.

The original logbook records three recurring categories:

- **Completed work:** Activities and development progress recorded for the period.
- **Planned work:** Tasks identified for subsequent development.
- **Impediments, risks and problems:** Challenges, dependencies or concerns affecting progress.

This structure helped distinguish what had already been achieved from what still needed attention.

## 02. Our Development Timeline

The following timeline summarises the activities recorded in the original AquaLink Agile Logbook.

### Phase 1 — Requirements and User Stories

**27–31 July 2026**

The early development period focused on clarifying AquaLink's requirements and identifying the functionality needed for the application.

This work helped establish the foundation for user stories, acceptance criteria and later development tasks.

The importance of this phase was understanding what the platform needed to accomplish before concentrating on how the screens would look.

### Phase 2 — UI/UX Design and Shared Foundations

**3–7 August 2026**

Attention moved towards the application's visual design and shared interface foundations.

The work included consideration of reusable components, consistent styling and the structure of the user experience.

This phase was especially relevant to my frontend responsibilities.

I wanted AquaLink to look coherent and feel like one application rather than a collection of disconnected pages.

### Phase 3 — Architecture and Data Requirements

**10–14 August 2026**

The project records show work on architecture, database requirements and relationships between system components.

The logbook also identifies dependencies relating to tank and sensor information, including readings, status and timestamps.

Importantly, documenting sensor data requirements did not mean that physical IoT hardware had already been integrated.

This phase helped clarify the difference between designing a system and connecting all of its underlying services.

### Phase 4 — Admin and Supplier Implementation

**17–28 August 2026**

Development progressed into the implementation of role-specific application screens.

The recorded work included Admin and Supplier functionality, such as dashboards, household information, plans, tanks, refill history and alerts.

For me, this stage represented the movement from requirements and designs into user-facing software.

It also required greater attention to visual consistency, navigation and how features connected to one another.

### Phase 5 — Shared Logic and Page Integration

**31 August–11 September 2026**

The development focus included shared application logic, navigation and consistency across screens.

As more features were introduced, it became important to ensure that different pages behaved as parts of the same application.

This reinforced one of my frontend lessons: a good user experience depends not only on individual screens, but also on how those screens work together.

### Phase 6 — Testing, Review and Stabilisation

**14 September–2 October 2026**

The logbook records work involving completion, regression testing, responsive behaviour and review of application functionality.

Testing was important because changes to shared components and navigation could affect features that had previously appeared to work.

This stage highlighted the need to review the application as a whole rather than evaluating every screen in isolation.

### Phase 7 — Finalisation and Presentation Preparation

**October 2026**

Later entries record attention to outstanding dependencies, documentation, final checks and presentation readiness.

The logbook distinguishes completed interface work from remaining technical integration activities.

The project continued evolving after this dated record, so the logbook should not be treated as a complete description of the application's later deployed state.

## 03. What the Logbook Revealed About Our Progress

<div class="story-panel">

<span class="eyebrow">A Critical Distinction</span>

### An implemented interface is not necessarily a fully integrated feature.

Our logbook records substantial progress on AquaLink's application screens.

It also identifies work that still required integration or verification, including persistent database workflows, external payment services and physical sensor connections.

Recognising this distinction helped us describe the project more accurately.

A screen may demonstrate the intended user experience while the underlying service is still being developed.

## 04. The Challenges Behind the Development

One of my biggest challenges as Project Lead was managing inconsistent team participation.

There were times when some team members did not engage as actively as I expected or failed to meet deadlines.

This frustrated me, particularly because I was also heavily involved in frontend development.

I wanted the project to progress, and I cared about the quality of what we were producing.

Balancing the responsibility of developing features with the responsibility of following up on other people's work was not always easy.

The experience taught me that progress tracking needs to be accompanied by clear communication and accountability.

A logbook can record a missed deadline or an incomplete task, but the project lead still needs to understand what caused the delay and how the team can move forward.

---

## 05. My Contribution to AquaLink

My role combined project coordination with substantial frontend development.

On the technical side, I worked on the application's appearance, layouts, interactions and overall user experience.

On the leadership side, I helped coordinate the project and monitor the progress of development activities.

These responsibilities often overlapped.

A frontend feature might depend on another part of the system being ready.

A delayed task could affect integration or testing.

A change to the design could require updates across multiple screens.

Through this experience, I began to understand software development as a coordinated process rather than a series of isolated coding activities.

## 07. My Personal Reflection

Looking back at the Agile Logbook, I can see how much work happened between our initial requirements and the application we eventually presented.

At the beginning, we were dealing with ideas, requirements and proposed features.

As development progressed, those ideas became screens, navigation flows, shared components and application functionality.

Because I worked extensively on the frontend, I became increasingly aware of how much attention was required to make the application consistent and usable.

But the logbook also reminds me that technical progress was only part of my responsibility.

I was leading a team.

There were moments when cooperation was difficult, deadlines were missed and the level of engagement did not meet my expectations.

Sometimes I became frustrated because I felt the responsibility of keeping the project moving while still completing my own work.

The experience challenged my understanding of leadership.

I learned that being committed to a project does not automatically mean everyone else will demonstrate that commitment in the same way.

I also learned that frustration alone does not improve team performance.

A project lead must communicate expectations, follow up on responsibilities and respond to difficulties constructively.

### What I Would Improve

If I were maintaining an Agile Logbook for another project, I would:

1. Link significant logbook entries to the relevant Trello cards.
2. Record the owner and status of each important task.
3. Document missed deadlines and the actions taken in response.
4. Record blockers and dependencies more consistently.
5. Link completed features to screenshots, commits or test evidence.
6. Distinguish implemented interfaces from fully verified integrations.
7. Include short retrospective notes explaining what the team learned.

These changes would make the logbook an even stronger record of project decisions and accountability.

## 08. What This Journey Means to Me

AquaLink began with a desire to use our education to improve the lives of the people who made that education possible.

The logbook documents the practical work required to pursue that ambition.

It shows that meaningful projects involve more than inspiration.

They require planning, technical decisions, collaboration, persistence and the willingness to confront difficulties.

For me, AquaLink became an experience in both software development and personal growth.

**I learned how to build interfaces, but I also learned how difficult and important it is to lead people towards a shared goal.**

That is what makes this logbook meaningful to me.
